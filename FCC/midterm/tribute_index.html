1918 – 2020 · Research Mathematician, NASA

Katherine Johnson

The human computer who calculated the path to the Moon

T−00:00

A trajectory, calculated by hand

Katherine Johnson joined NASA's Flight Research Division in 1953, at a time when the agency's computers were rooms full of women doing arithmetic no machine could yet be trusted to do alone. She specialized in orbital mechanics: the geometry of a body falling around a planet, plotted step by step until it lands exactly where it's supposed to.

1961

Freedom 7

She calculated the trajectory for Alan Shepard's 1961 flight, the first spaceflight by an American. It was the kind of work that had to be right the first time, plotted on paper with pencil and slide rule before anyone trusted it to a machine.

1962

"Get the girl to check the numbers"

Before John Glenn's orbital flight aboard Friendship 7, NASA had begun using electronic computers to plot trajectories — but Glenn didn't trust them until Johnson had verified the math herself. He asked for her by name. She spent a day and a half re-running the equations, and the mission flew.

1969

Apollo 11

Her calculations helped synchronize the Apollo Lunar Module with the orbiting Command and Service Module, work that was essential to bringing astronauts safely to the Moon and back. She stayed at NASA through the Space Shuttle program, retiring in 1986 after thirty-three years of work that quietly kept people alive.

2015

Recognition, decades later

In 2015, President Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom. In 2016, NASA named a new research facility the Katherine G. Johnson Computational Research Facility, and her story reached a wider audience through the book and film Hidden Figures.
